How to use this guide

This is an engagement-oriented curriculum, not a catalogue of commands. Read Modules 1–3 before selecting a specialist path, then return to Modules 8–10 to connect the technical result to detection, reporting, and retesting. Every practical exercise should produce evidence that another authorized reviewer can reproduce without relying on the operator’s memory or an AI-generated narrative.

Foundation track

Modules 1–3 establish authority, objectives, reconnaissance, validation discipline, and evidence. They are mandatory even when a specialist performs only web, cloud, or identity testing.

Specialist tracks

Modules 4–7 cover application, infrastructure, cloud, identity, privilege, and movement. Select only the paths authorized by the engagement and supported by the laboratory or target environment.

Validation track

Module 8 converts relevant adversary behavior into observable, reversible tests. It separates prevention, telemetry, analytic coverage, alert handling, and response outcomes.

Assistance and delivery

Modules 9–10 define when AI and MCP can assist, how tools are constrained, how labs are designed, and how evidence becomes a report, defensive handoff, and retest decision.

Required engagement record

Maintain one record containing authorization and ROE, target inventory, objective-to-test mapping, operator timeline, tool versions and configuration, raw outputs, evidence references, changes made, cleanup confirmation, findings, detection observations, reviewer decisions, limitations, and retest state. A result that cannot be traced to this record remains a lead, not a defensible conclusion.

Module 1 — Mission, authorization, and methodology

6 topics

The engagement contract is a security control. A technically successful test can still be a failed engagement if it exceeds authority, disrupts production, mishandles data, or cannot explain business impact.

Red team vs. penetration test vs. vulnerability assessment

A vulnerability assessment identifies and prioritizes likely weaknesses. A penetration test validates whether selected weaknesses are exploitable and what access or impact follows. A red-team engagement evaluates whether people, process, technology, and detections can resist a threat-informed objective across a realistic attack chain. These activities overlap, but they do not have interchangeable success criteria.

Choose the least intrusive assessment capable of answering the stakeholder’s question. If the question is “which internet-facing services need remediation,” start with exposure and vulnerability validation. If it is “can a realistic actor reach a protected business process without being contained,” a controlled red-team or purple-team exercise may be appropriate.

1200km path: PT Tools & Techniques · CTI to detection · AdversaryGraph vs. Atomic Red Team

Scope, authorization, and rules of engagement

NIST defines rules of engagement as detailed constraints established before a security test that give the team authority to conduct defined activities. A professional ROE names in-scope assets and identities, permitted techniques, test windows, third-party dependencies, source addresses, data handling, notification paths, incident handling, stop conditions, and the authority that may resume a paused test.

  • Use explicit asset identifiers; “the company domain” is not enough when SaaS, CDN, or shared hosting is involved.
  • Separate permitted discovery, validation, exploitation, persistence, social engineering, denial-of-service, and data-access actions.
  • Record emergency contacts and a verifiable kill switch before intrusive testing.
  • Obtain additional authorization for cloud tenants, managed providers, or other third-party infrastructure.

Boundary: public visibility is not authorization. A Shodan result, exposed login page, bug-bounty listing, or discovered IP does not grant permission beyond the applicable program policy and written scope.

Primary reference: NIST SP 800-115 · Site: 1200km data-handling policy

Objectives, threat model, and success criteria

Start with a business decision and a threat model, not a favorite exploit. Define the protected process, likely adversaries, assumed starting position, target actions, control hypotheses, and acceptable proof. Threat intelligence can provide known behaviors, but the plan must be tailored to the environment’s telemetry and defensive objectives.

A useful objective is measurable: “test whether a standard external user can obtain access to restricted billing data and whether identity, endpoint, and SIEM controls detect or contain the path.” “Compromise the network” is too broad to guide safe execution or meaningful reporting.

Operational security, deconfliction, and safety

Engagement OPSEC protects both the client and the integrity of the test. Use dedicated infrastructure and accounts, prevent test data from escaping the scope, log operator actions, and coordinate with a small white team that can distinguish exercise activity from a real incident without pre-alerting every defender.

Deconfliction is not concealment at any cost. Real malicious activity, unexpected sensitive data, production instability, or a scope ambiguity should trigger the documented pause-and-escalate process. Safety takes priority over scenario realism.

Evidence, reproducibility, and chain of custody

Decide before testing what evidence is required to prove each objective: timestamps, tool versions, commands or requests, target identifiers, screenshots, relevant logs, observed controls, and cleanup confirmation. Store evidence in the approved location with access control and retention limits.

Every finding should distinguish observation, reproduction, inference, and impact. A scanner alert is an observation; a controlled reproduction is validation; a claim that the flaw enables business-process compromise is an inference until the authorized path is demonstrated or otherwise supported.

Applied workflow — design an engagement that can be defended

The first deliverable is not a scan: it is a signed, testable agreement between the sponsor, system owner, white team, operators, and evidence consumers. The following workflow turns a broad request such as “red team our environment” into bounded decisions and observable outcomes.

  1. Translate the request into a business decision. Identify what the sponsor needs to decide after the engagement: accept a risk, fund a control, validate a response process, compare detection capability, or approve a release.
  2. Name the protected operation. Describe the business process, sensitive action, data class, critical identities, trust boundaries, and unacceptable outcomes. This prevents the assessment from becoming an infrastructure-only exercise.
  3. Define the assumed starting point. External unauthenticated, standard workforce user, compromised endpoint, contractor identity, or cloud workload are different tests. State which access, knowledge, and credentials the operator receives.
  4. Build the authority matrix. Map each target class to discovery, authentication testing, exploitation, social engineering, persistence, data access, movement, impact simulation, and cleanup permissions. Mark prohibited actions explicitly.
  5. Write objective-level success and stop criteria. Define the minimum evidence that answers each objective and the event that stops further action. Reaching a synthetic marker may be sufficient; accessing real customer records rarely is.
  6. Perform a technical and safety dry run. Verify source infrastructure, time synchronization, logging, emergency communication, test identities, target allowlists, backups, snapshots, rate limits, and cleanup procedures in a lab or staging environment.
  7. Open the evidence ledger before execution. Assign identifiers to objectives, actions, artifacts, findings, and changes so every screenshot, scan, request, and defensive observation can be traced to a reviewed decision.
  8. Close formally. Confirm cleanup, revoke test access, inventory residual artifacts, transfer evidence, document limitations, and obtain owner acknowledgement before treating the engagement as complete.

Minimum artifacts

  • Authorization letter and signed ROE
  • Objective and success-criteria matrix
  • Asset, identity, provider, and exclusion list
  • Contact, escalation, pause, and resume plan
  • Evidence, retention, and data-destruction plan
  • Change and cleanup ledger

Safe lab exercise

Create two fictional engagement briefs for the same vulnerable lab: one vulnerability assessment and one threat-informed red-team exercise. Compare authority, success criteria, evidence, test depth, defensive participation, and reporting. The technical target stays unchanged; the mission changes the work.

Example scenario

A SaaS provider wants to know whether a compromised standard account can reach restricted billing exports without prevention or a triaged alert. The authorized proof is access to a synthetic record and a correlated defensive timeline—not downloading the production dataset.

AI assistance boundary

An assistant may compare the ROE with a proposed test plan, identify ambiguity, draft checklists, and normalize notes. It must not expand scope, infer permission, select a real target, or approve an intrusive action. Only the named authority can change the engagement.

Evidence gate: do not begin active collection until each target can be tied to written authority, a named owner, a permitted action class, an evidence requirement, a stop condition, and a cleanup owner.

Module 2 — Reconnaissance and attack-surface mapping

5 topics

Reconnaissance should produce a scoped, attributed asset model—not an unreviewed pile of domains, ports, and screenshots.

Passive vs. active reconnaissance

Passive reconnaissance uses existing public or authorized data—DNS history, certificate transparency, repositories, documentation, exposure indexes, and supplied inventories—without directly probing the target. Active reconnaissance sends traffic to in-scope systems to confirm hosts, services, technologies, and reachable paths.

Passive sources can be stale, shared, or misattributed; active results can affect production and alert defenders. Label the collection method and timestamp, preserve the source, and validate ownership before moving an observation into the target inventory. The HexStrike OSINT exposure-map article demonstrates how a seed can expand while still requiring analyst review.

1200km path: OSINT & Reconnaissance · Shodan with HexStrike

Build an attributed asset and identity model

For each candidate asset, record ownership confidence, hostname or cloud resource ID, address, environment, business service, exposure, authentication boundary, technology evidence, and source. Treat wildcard DNS, CDN endpoints, shared cloud addresses, abandoned records, subsidiaries, and third-party SaaS as attribution problems rather than automatic scope additions.

Identity is part of the attack surface: test accounts, service principals, APIs, federation, exposed credentials, and support workflows can matter more than ports. Keep discovered identities and secrets out of notes or AI prompts unless the engagement’s handling rules explicitly permit them.

Network discovery, service enumeration, and Nmap

Network enumeration progresses from host discovery to port state, service fingerprinting, protocol-aware checks, and manual confirmation. Rate, timing, UDP coverage, source location, and network controls all affect what a scan can observe. “Closed,” “filtered,” and “no response” are different evidence states.

The four-part Nmap guide series covers core scanning, service and OS detection, firewall behavior, and NSE. Use safe scripts first, pin tool versions, save machine-readable output, and never equate a service banner or automated version match with a confirmed vulnerability.

Articles: Nmap Part 1 · Part 2 · NSE scripts

Turn recon into hypotheses

Prioritize paths using business criticality, exposure, authentication, likely attack preconditions, observed defensive controls, and the objective—not merely the number of open ports. A mature recon output is a reviewed attack-surface map plus questions to validate, such as whether an exposed management service is truly internet reachable or belongs to a shared provider.

AI can summarize large scan results or correlate naming patterns, but it must not invent asset ownership, product versions, or exploitability. Keep the original scan artifacts and require each AI-produced claim to cite the underlying host, port, request, or source record.

Applied workflow — build an attributed attack-surface map

Effective reconnaissance is an evidence-reconciliation process. It joins supplied inventory with public observations and carefully controlled active validation, while preventing shared infrastructure, stale DNS, acquired companies, and third-party services from silently entering scope.

  1. Normalize the seed set. Import approved domains, CIDRs, cloud accounts, applications, APIs, mobile backends, identity providers, repositories, subsidiaries, and third parties. Preserve the system of record and owner for every seed.
  2. Collect passive observations by source. Record DNS and certificate names, registration data, public documents, code references, storage endpoints, exposure-engine observations, and historical records with collection time and source URL.
  3. Resolve attribution before expansion. Use DNS chains, certificates, organization data, application content, cloud identifiers, supplied inventory, and owner confirmation. Assign confidence and status: confirmed, likely, ambiguous, shared-provider, third-party, or excluded.
  4. Plan active validation. Group targets by risk and protocol; define source address, scan rate, timing, TCP/UDP coverage, retry policy, safe probes, and expected monitoring. Start narrow and increase breadth only when results and system health justify it.
  5. Fingerprint at multiple layers. Separate network reachability, protocol behavior, TLS and HTTP characteristics, product evidence, authentication boundary, and application identity. Do not let one banner populate all fields.
  6. Correlate identities and trust. Record federation endpoints, exposed administrative interfaces, service principals, API documentation, password-reset flows, support channels, and machine identities without collecting real credentials unnecessarily.
  7. Convert observations into hypotheses. Examples include an orphaned DNS record, an internet-reachable management plane, a legacy authentication path, an exposed artifact repository, or a version that requires confirmation.
  8. Review deltas with owners. Distinguish new assets from newly observed assets, document uncertainty, and route confirmed inventory gaps to the accountable team before vulnerability testing.

Asset record fields

  • Canonical identifier and aliases
  • Owner, business service, environment, and criticality
  • DNS, address, cloud, CDN, and provider relationships
  • Exposure, ports, protocols, and authentication
  • Technology evidence and confidence
  • Source, first/last seen, scope, and review state

Tool roles

Use DNS and certificate tools for naming evidence, Shodan/Censys-style indexes for historical exposure leads, Nmap for controlled network validation, HTTP and TLS clients for application-layer evidence, and Wireshark for protocol confirmation. Each tool answers a narrower question than “is this vulnerable?”

Safe lab exercise

Build a three-host private lab with a reverse proxy, one intentionally stale DNS entry, and one shared service. Start with only the parent domain and supplied inventory. Produce an attributed map that correctly separates confirmed, ambiguous, and out-of-scope observations.

1200km worked path

Follow the Nmap-assisted analysis guide, the Shodan + HexStrike workflow, and the OSINT exposure-map case study. Treat every enrichment result as a reviewable lead.

Evidence gate: an asset may enter active testing only when its attribution, scope state, owner or responsible provider, resolution path, collection time, and permitted probe class are recorded.

Module 3 — Vulnerability discovery and validation

5 topics

A finding becomes defensible when the affected component, version, preconditions, reproducibility, control context, and impact are supported by evidence.

Scanner output is a lead, not a verdict

Automated scanners provide breadth and repeatability, but banners, heuristic matches, generic checks, and incomplete authentication create false positives and false negatives. Preserve the plugin or template ID, evidence, request, response, confidence, and scanner version; then validate the exact deployed product and affected condition.

Use multiple tools only when they answer different questions. Duplicating five scanners does not replace manual validation. The PT Tools library separates discovery, web testing, credential auditing, and exploitation tools so the method drives selection rather than the other way around.

Manual validation and minimum necessary proof

Validate with the least harmful action that proves the condition. Confirm the request path, affected identity, required privileges, response difference, and repeatability. Prefer a benign marker, read-only query, or test object over sensitive-data access, destructive modification, or persistence.

Document why the proof is sufficient and where testing stopped. A report should never imply that code execution, privilege escalation, lateral movement, or data access occurred if only a version banner or scanner signature was observed.

CVE and exploit research

Correlate vendor advisories, NVD records, CISA KEV, exploit references, EPSS, product configuration, and deployment context. Public proof-of-concept code may be incomplete, unsafe, modified, or irrelevant to the installed version. Review source, isolate it, understand side effects, and test against a lab replica before any authorized production validation.

The Vulnerability Research syllabus covers root-cause and exploit-development concepts; the threat–vulnerability–risk distinction explains why a CVE score alone does not determine priority.

Exploitability, reachability, and business impact

Prioritize using evidence: affected version, reachable path, authentication requirements, privilege gained, exploit maturity, known exploitation, compensating controls, asset importance, and blast radius. CVSS communicates severity characteristics; it is not proof that an organization is exposed or compromised.

1200km guide: CVSS v4.0 Practical Field Guide · CVE/CVSS intelligence workflow

Applied workflow — turn a scanner lead into a validated finding

Validation connects a technical observation to the exact affected component, reachable condition, authorized proof, and business consequence. The objective is not to maximize exploit execution; it is to reduce uncertainty with the minimum necessary change to the target.

  1. Preserve the original lead. Save the scanner engine and version, policy, template or plugin ID, target, timestamp, request and response evidence, confidence, severity, and any authentication state.
  2. Confirm asset identity. Verify that the responding service is the scoped asset rather than a CDN, WAF, proxy, shared address, honeypot, or redirected third party. Record the exact hostname, address, virtual host, route, and tenant context.
  3. Identify the deployed component. Correlate vendor metadata, package or image inventory, authenticated configuration, software bill of materials, protocol behavior, headers, and owner knowledge. Treat banners as evidence with limited confidence.
  4. Evaluate the affected condition. Read the vendor advisory and primary vulnerability record. Check fixed and affected versions, configuration prerequisites, platform, authentication, privileges, attack vector, mitigations, and whether the vulnerable code path is enabled and reachable.
  5. Design minimum proof. Define a reversible request or state change that distinguishes vulnerable from non-vulnerable behavior without accessing unrelated data, executing destructive code, creating persistence, or increasing blast radius.
  6. Validate controls and telemetry. Record WAF, EDR, identity, segmentation, runtime, and logging behavior. A compensating control may reduce exploitability without removing the underlying vulnerable condition; report both.
  7. Determine consequence and confidence. Trace the validated primitive to realistic privilege, data, service, or attack-path impact. Separate demonstrated impact from plausible impact and assign confidence to each conclusion.
  8. Stop, clean up, and retest safely. Remove test data or accounts, restore state, notify the owner, provide a remediation test, and retain the original evidence so closure can be compared with the same condition.

Finding evidence packet

  • Affected asset, component, version, and configuration
  • Primary advisory and vulnerability references
  • Preconditions and authorized reproduction
  • Sanitized request/response or state evidence
  • Observed controls, impact, confidence, and limitations
  • Cleanup and remediation-validation procedure

Decision sources

Vendor advisories establish the affected condition; NVD and CVSS describe standardized characteristics; CISA KEV records known exploitation for listed vulnerabilities; EPSS estimates exploitation probability; local inventory, reachability, controls, and asset context determine organizational exposure and priority.

Safe lab exercise

Scan an intentionally vulnerable Ubuntu or Metasploitable host, select three alerts, and build evidence packets. Confirm one, reject one false positive, and leave one unresolved because version or configuration evidence is missing. Explain why uncertainty is an acceptable result.

AI assistance boundary

AI may compare version ranges, summarize advisories, cluster duplicate alerts, and draft validation questions. It may not declare exploitability from a CVE match, choose or execute proof-of-concept code, or convert probability scores into factual compromise claims.

Evidence gate: promote a lead to a finding only when the scoped asset, affected component and condition, reproduction evidence, impact statement, confidence, control context, and cleanup state are independently reviewable.

Module 4 — Web applications and APIs

5 topics

Use a repeatable testing model that covers architecture, identity, authorization, sessions, input handling, business logic, APIs, clients, and deployment—not just an automated crawl.

Map the application before testing it

The OWASP Web Security Testing Guide provides a structured framework for application and web-service testing. Begin with architecture, entry points, trust boundaries, roles, data flows, technologies, and environment differences. Record a versioned test inventory so omissions and retests are visible.

For each test, define the identity used, request, expected security property, observed behavior, evidence, and cleanup. Avoid reporting generic missing headers or version strings as high-impact vulnerabilities without a credible path and context.

Primary: OWASP WSTG · 1200km: Secure Code / Application Security

Authentication, session, and authorization testing

Test registration, recovery, MFA, session lifecycle, token handling, role definitions, object-level authorization, and administrative workflows. The central question is whether each server-side action enforces the intended identity and permission—not whether the user interface hides a button.

Build a role–action–object matrix and test allowed and denied paths with controlled accounts. Preserve evidence without retaining real user tokens or data. Reauthentication and step-up controls should be evaluated for sensitive operations.

Input handling and business-logic abuse

Injection, cross-site scripting, request forgery, file handling, deserialization, path traversal, and server-side request forgery require context-specific validation. Business-logic flaws often arise from valid operations used in an unintended sequence, at an unintended frequency, or under the wrong identity.

Fuzzing can expand coverage, but manual reasoning is required to understand state, invariants, and impact. Rate-limit tests and workflow manipulation must stay within the ROE to avoid availability or financial effects.

Burp Suite, ZAP, browser tools, and evidence

An intercepting proxy supports request capture, replay, comparison, and controlled mutation; browser developer tools reveal client behavior; scanners add breadth; command-line tools support repeatability. The Burp Scanner guide and Burp interface-testing tutorial show how to turn proxy observations into structured evidence.

For an AI-assisted lab workflow, see Cursor + HexStrike + Burp MCP. Treat the model as a planning and interpretation layer; the proxy history remains the authoritative record.

Applied workflow — test one business transaction end to end

The strongest web assessment follows data and authority through a complete transaction. It tests the browser or client, edge controls, application, API, identity provider, asynchronous workers, storage, and audit trail as one system instead of producing disconnected endpoint findings.

  1. Model architecture and trust. Identify public and internal origins, reverse proxies, CDNs, WAFs, identity providers, APIs, queues, storage, administrative planes, mobile clients, webhooks, and third-party integrations.
  2. Create controlled identities and data. Use at least two standard users from separate tenants or organizations plus an approved privileged role. Seed synthetic records with unique markers so cross-user and cross-tenant access can be proven safely.
  3. Capture a valid baseline. Record the complete transaction, including redirects, tokens, cookies, headers, object identifiers, API calls, state changes, background jobs, notifications, and resulting audit entries.
  4. Build the authorization matrix. For every action, compare unauthenticated, own-object, other-user, other-tenant, lower-role, expired-session, and administrative contexts. Validate on the server side; client-side restrictions are not authorization evidence.
  5. Test session and token lifecycle. Evaluate issuance, storage, rotation, expiry, logout, revocation, device changes, MFA and recovery, step-up authentication, refresh behavior, and replay within the allowed accounts.
  6. Test input and state transitions. Exercise type, size, encoding, sequence, concurrency, file, URL, and object-reference boundaries. Define rate and cost limits before automation, and stop when a test could affect availability or external systems.
  7. Trace server-side effects. Verify where data is stored, whether jobs or webhooks execute, what permissions they use, how errors are handled, and whether security-relevant events reach an audit or detection pipeline.
  8. Reproduce and minimize. Reduce each candidate to the smallest request sequence that proves the violated security property, sanitize secrets, preserve both allowed and denied comparisons, clean synthetic data, and retest the fix.

Coverage inventory

  • Roles, tenants, objects, actions, and expected decisions
  • Authentication, recovery, MFA, and session states
  • Web, REST, GraphQL, WebSocket, and webhook surfaces
  • Uploads, downloads, exports, and asynchronous jobs
  • Administrative and support workflows
  • Security logging, notifications, and response behavior

Evidence pattern

Preserve a sanitized baseline request, one controlled mutation, the response and resulting state, identity and tenant context, timestamps, proxy history reference, server or audit event where available, expected behavior, observed behavior, and cleanup confirmation.

Safe lab exercise

Use an intentionally vulnerable web lab with two tenants. Select one export or object-update transaction, build the role–action–object matrix, validate an authorization weakness using synthetic records, then add a server-side check and demonstrate the denied retest.

Tools and interpretation

Use Burp Suite or ZAP for history and controlled replay, browser tools for client state, API clients for reproducible collections, and application logs for server confirmation. AI may compare flows and suggest untested states; it cannot prove a server-side effect absent evidence.

Evidence gate: report a web or API finding only when the tested identity, tenant, object, action, expected security property, observed result, downstream effect, and reproducible sanitized evidence are explicit.

Module 5 — Cloud, containers, and Kubernetes

5 topics

Cloud testing combines provider authorization, identity, control-plane APIs, workload exposure, data paths, and audit evidence. A hostname scan alone is not a cloud assessment.

Provider policy, tenant boundaries, and shared responsibility

Confirm the cloud provider’s current testing policy, tenant and subscription IDs, regions, managed-service restrictions, and prohibited actions. Distinguish customer configuration from provider infrastructure, and do not treat shared service endpoints as owned targets.

Map internet exposure, control-plane permissions, workload identities, secrets, storage, network policy, logging, and cross-account trust. Use resource IDs and audit timestamps rather than ambiguous IP-only attribution.

Cloud identity and privilege paths

Review users, groups, roles, service accounts, managed identities, federation, OAuth grants, API keys, conditional access, and permission boundaries. Effective privilege is the combination of direct grants, inherited roles, trust relationships, and the ability to modify another identity or workload.

The GCP penetration-testing guide provides a platform-specific workflow. Pair it with the identity attack-surface model so permissions are analyzed as paths, not isolated findings.

Kubernetes and container assessment

Assess exposed control planes, authentication, RBAC, admission, workload identities, service accounts, secrets, network policy, image provenance, host mounts, privileged workloads, and escape paths. A cluster is both an API authorization system and a workload runtime.

The Black-Box Kubernetes Penetration Testing Playbook organizes discovery and validation while preserving a black-box evidence boundary. Use disposable lab clusters for intrusive container and privilege tests.

Cloud evidence, cost, and cleanup

Capture provider audit events, resource diffs, identity decisions, and relevant requests. Tag test resources, set budgets and expiry, rotate temporary credentials, delete created objects, restore policy changes, and verify cleanup through the control plane. Report residual risk if a created token, snapshot, role, or public object cannot be conclusively removed.

Applied workflow — analyze a cloud privilege path without losing tenant context

Cloud assessments should be resource- and identity-aware from the first API call. The same public IP can represent provider infrastructure for many customers, while a low-visibility permission on one role can control a sensitive workload. Resource IDs, effective permissions, trust, and audit events are stronger evidence than network appearance alone.

  1. Establish the authorized control plane. Record organization, tenant, account, subscription, project, region, cluster, provider policy, test identity, allowed services, cost limits, and prohibited provider-layer actions.
  2. Build a resource and ownership graph. Inventory public endpoints, workloads, storage, databases, functions, registries, secrets, networks, clusters, CI/CD identities, SaaS integrations, and accountable teams using immutable provider identifiers.
  3. Calculate effective identity. Trace direct and inherited roles, groups, resource policies, federation, workload identity, service-account impersonation, permission boundaries, conditions, delegated administration, OAuth consent, and cross-account trust.
  4. Map reachable data and execution paths. Ask which identity can read secrets, alter code or images, attach a role, modify a policy, create a key, invoke a workload, access metadata, change logging, expose storage, or move into another account.
  5. Review workload boundaries. For containers and Kubernetes, connect RBAC, service accounts, admission, security context, host access, network policy, secret mounts, image provenance, node permissions, and cloud workload identity.
  6. Validate with reversible actions. Prefer policy simulation, read-only inventory, denied requests, synthetic objects, short-lived tagged resources, and test identities. Any permission change or resource creation requires a documented rollback and owner.
  7. Observe the defensive path. Correlate cloud audit, identity, network, workload, Kubernetes audit, runtime, and SIEM events. Verify event latency, actor attribution, source context, rule behavior, and analyst routing.
  8. Reconcile and clean up. Delete tagged resources, revoke sessions and temporary keys, restore policies, confirm logging, check costs, compare inventory with the pre-test baseline, and retain provider event IDs as evidence.

Cloud evidence packet

  • Tenant/account/project and immutable resource ID
  • Test principal and effective permission chain
  • Policy, trust, condition, and workload context
  • Sanitized API request and provider event ID
  • Before/after state and defensive observations
  • Cost, expiry, credential, and cleanup confirmation

Identity references

Use the AWS IAM overview, Entra ID overview, Google Cloud IAM guide, and Kubernetes RBAC guide to interpret provider-specific objects without collapsing them into one generic “cloud role.”

Safe lab exercise

Create a disposable cloud project with a public test service, private storage, one intentionally over-permissioned workload identity, audit logging, budget alerts, and an expiry tag. Demonstrate the privilege relationship with a synthetic object, confirm the audit trail, remediate the policy, and prove cleanup.

Worked material

Use the GCP testing guide, Kubernetes playbook, and AdversaryGraph cloud investigation case study as complementary offensive, platform, and evidence perspectives.

Evidence gate: a cloud finding must identify the exact tenant, resource, principal, effective-permission chain, reachable action, provider evidence, business consequence, defensive signal, and cleanup state.

Module 6 — Active Directory and identity attack paths

5 topics

Identity testing is relationship analysis: users, machines, services, certificates, policies, trusts, and delegated rights combine into paths that single-object reviews miss.

Directory and trust enumeration

Build a model of domains, forests, trusts, organizational units, privileged groups, service accounts, local administration, sessions, delegation, certificate services, and identity synchronization. Review collection permissions and protect directory datasets because graph exports can reveal sensitive attack paths.

1200km reference: Active Directory overview · AD trusts · Authorized AD lab setup

Kerberos and credential-service attack paths

Understand tickets, service principal names, delegation, preauthentication, credential material, and the difference between a protocol feature and a risky configuration. Kerberoasting, AS-REP roasting, ticket abuse, and replication-related paths each require distinct preconditions and produce different defensive evidence.

Use the Kerberoasting attack reference alongside Kerberoasting detection guidance. Offensive validation is incomplete if it cannot tell defenders which logs and observable behaviors should have existed.

ADCS, delegation, ACLs, and control relationships

Certificate templates, enrollment endpoints, object permissions, resource-based delegation, Group Policy, and replication rights can create privilege paths without a software vulnerability. Validate the exact principal, right, template condition, and reachable service; avoid generic “ADCS vulnerable” conclusions.

The ADCS primer, ESC1 reference, and controlled HexStrike ADCS ESC8 lab connect architecture, evidence, and a lab-only AI-assisted workflow.

Identity laboratory progression

Start with an isolated AD domain, then add certificate services, Linux identity, Entra ID, and a logging stack. Snapshot each stage, use synthetic identities, document normal traffic, and validate one attack path plus its detection before chaining scenarios.

Labs: Lab architecture · Entra ID · Linux identity · Domain compromise scenario

Applied workflow — prove one identity control path and its detection

Identity environments are graphs of authority. A standard user may influence a service account, group, certificate template, application, device, or synchronized cloud identity that ultimately controls a protected resource. The assessment should explain the complete path and the conditions that break it.

  1. Define identity tiers and protected actions. Identify workforce, service, device, application, privileged, emergency, and external identities; authentication systems; trust boundaries; and the business operations that require stronger protection.
  2. Collect the directory model with minimum privilege. Record domains, forests, tenants, trusts, OUs, groups, roles, sessions, SPNs, delegation, certificate services, synchronization, local administration, policies, and object-control rights.
  3. Normalize control relationships. Distinguish membership, ownership, write rights, delegation, impersonation, certificate enrollment, password or key reset, consent, session control, local execution, and policy modification. “Can reach” is not the same as “can control.”
  4. Select a path tied to an objective. Prioritize paths from the assumed starting identity toward a protected action using preconditions, current sessions, control reliability, operational impact, and expected telemetry—not graph length alone.
  5. Validate each edge independently. Confirm the exact principal, target, permission, protocol, service state, authentication condition, and resulting authorization decision. Use test objects and reversible changes where possible.
  6. Observe protocol and control evidence. Correlate directory changes, Kerberos, LDAP, certificate, endpoint, cloud identity, and SIEM events. Note which edge was prevented, merely logged, detected, or investigated.
  7. Remediate the relationship. Remove unnecessary rights, redesign delegation, harden templates, isolate tiers, rotate affected credentials, reduce standing privilege, and validate that required administration still works.
  8. Retest path closure. Re-run the same edge with the same test identity, confirm the denial and logs, then recalculate whether an alternate path remains.

Path record

  • Starting identity and authentication assurance
  • Each principal, object, permission, and protocol edge
  • Required service, session, template, or policy state
  • Authorized proof and before/after evidence
  • Expected and observed telemetry per edge
  • Remediation owner and alternate-path review

Protocol study path

Start with Kerberos, LDAP, AD trusts, and ADCS. Then study attack and detection pairs such as Kerberoasting and its detection guide.

Safe lab exercise

Deploy the AD lab with synthetic users, one service account, a deliberately risky delegation or certificate relationship, endpoint logging, directory auditing, and a SIEM. Validate one edge, observe the events, remediate it, and prove the path is closed.

Hybrid extension

Add an Entra ID lab and trace one synchronized or federated identity path. Keep on-premises object rights, cloud roles, OAuth grants, conditional access, device state, and token lifecycle as distinct evidence layers.

Evidence gate: an identity path is validated only when every edge has a named principal, controlled object, effective right, protocol or service condition, resulting action, telemetry record, and remediation decision.

Module 7 — Privilege, lateral movement, and controlled impact

5 topics

Post-exploitation proves paths and defensive visibility with the minimum necessary action. It is not permission to collect everything reachable.

Privilege escalation as a path, not a checklist

Review service configuration, file and registry permissions, scheduled tasks, tokens, kernel or application exposure, cloud metadata, local groups, delegated rights, and secrets. Tie every candidate to the current identity, platform, version, precondition, and expected privilege transition.

Use a lab replica when an exploit risks a crash or state corruption. The ATT&CK exploitation-for-privilege-escalation page provides behavioral context; the report still needs target-specific evidence.

Credential access and secret handling

Credential testing may involve password policy, exposed secrets, hashes, tokens, cookies, keys, service credentials, or memory access. Define whether collection, cracking, replay, and storage are authorized separately. Prefer synthetic accounts and test secrets wherever possible.

Password-recovery tooling has legitimate uses but creates sensitive artifacts. The HexStrike hub includes ZIP, Office, and PDF recovery labs; apply them only to authorized recovery samples and keep hashes, wordlists, and recovered material within the evidence policy.

Pivoting and lateral movement

Pivoting changes the network vantage point; lateral movement changes the accessed system or identity. Before either, define approved routes, target tiers, protocols, source addresses, credential use, and segmentation expectations. Log each hop so defenders can reconstruct the path.

The single-target to subnet-compromise lab illustrates AI-assisted chaining in a controlled environment. Its lesson is the path and telemetry—not permission to automate movement in a production network.

Impact simulation, persistence, and cleanup

Use reversible proof: a benign marker, test record, synthetic file, controlled account, or pre-agreed simulation. Denial of service, destructive actions, ransomware-like encryption, production persistence, bulk data access, and safety-system interaction require exceptional explicit authority and often should be represented rather than executed.

Maintain a change ledger and remove payloads, users, keys, tasks, services, files, rules, tunnels, cloud resources, and test data. Verify cleanup independently and disclose anything that cannot be confirmed.

Applied workflow — validate a post-exploitation path with minimum impact

Post-exploitation answers a bounded question about privilege, reachability, trust, or defensive response. It should not become open-ended collection. Every transition needs a reason, a permitted proof, a telemetry expectation, and a cleanup action before execution.

  1. Reconfirm the current state. Record the controlled identity, host or workload, integrity or privilege level, network vantage point, acquired capability, evidence source, and the objective that requires another step.
  2. Enumerate locally before acting. Review platform, patch and configuration evidence, execution boundaries, services, permissions, scheduled activity, secrets locations, network routes, and defensive controls. Avoid broad collection unrelated to the objective.
  3. Rank privilege transitions. Prefer configuration and delegated-right paths that can be proven reversibly. Evaluate required version, access, interaction, stability, likely side effects, and telemetry before considering exploit-based transitions.
  4. Control credential handling. Use synthetic or engagement-specific credentials when possible. If collection of real material is authorized, minimize fields, encrypt evidence, restrict access, prohibit reuse, rotate promptly, and document destruction.
  5. Plan each pivot. Identify source, destination, protocol, port, credential or token, expected segmentation decision, defensive signal, transfer requirement, and rollback. Do not use a newly reachable subnet as automatic permission to scan it.
  6. Demonstrate controlled impact. Use a unique marker, synthetic record, harmless test file, approved account, or pre-staged canary. Record the proof and stop at the objective instead of collecting additional data.
  7. Pair execution with observation. Capture endpoint, identity, network, application, cloud, and SIEM evidence. Note prevention, logging, alert creation, enrichment, analyst triage, containment, and communication separately.
  8. Perform independent cleanup verification. Remove accounts, keys, tokens, tasks, services, files, routes, listeners, tunnels, firewall rules, payloads, cloud objects, and test records; then have a second reviewer compare the change ledger with actual state.

Transition record

  • Objective and reason for continuing
  • Source identity, asset, privilege, and capability
  • Destination and approved protocol or action
  • Preconditions, expected result, and stop point
  • Operator and defensive timestamps
  • Changes, artifacts, secrets, and cleanup owner

When to simulate

Represent rather than execute actions that could interrupt service, encrypt or delete data, affect safety, trigger financial transactions, send external messages, expose regulated records, create durable persistence, or make recovery uncertain. The report must state that impact was simulated.

Safe lab exercise

Use the isolated subnet-compromise lab to document one privilege transition and one approved pivot. Replace unrestricted compromise goals with a synthetic marker, a route allowlist, a change ledger, and a second-person cleanup review.

Defensive handoff

For each transition, provide the exact behavior, ATT&CK mapping, affected control, available data source, observed event, analytic result, false-positive considerations, containment opportunity, and retest procedure. Tool names alone are not detection requirements.

Evidence gate: continue beyond the current foothold only when the next transition is explicitly in scope, necessary for an objective, reversible, observable, assigned a cleanup action, and supported by a defined stop point.

Module 8 — Threat-informed adversary emulation and purple teaming

5 topics

Threat-informed testing asks whether relevant behaviors are prevented, observed, detected, investigated, and contained—not merely whether an operator can run a tool.

Build an ATT&CK-based emulation plan

MITRE’s adversary-emulation plans use public threat reporting and ATT&CK to model representative behavior. Select techniques supported by current evidence and relevant to the organization, document assumptions and substitutions, then map each action to a defensive hypothesis and expected telemetry.

ATT&CK is not a linear checklist and matrix coverage is not equivalent to security. Use the ATT&CK working guide and Threat Matrix to build an actor-informed layer without claiming unvalidated coverage.

Primary: MITRE adversary-emulation plans

Atomic tests, CALDERA, and scenario chains

Atomic tests isolate a technique for repeatable control validation; CALDERA and other emulation platforms can sequence post-compromise behaviors. Start with one observable action, verify telemetry and cleanup, then expand into a chain. Automation does not remove the need for ROE, target controls, or human approval.

1200km path: AdversaryGraph Attack Simulation · Attack–Defense Simulation Framework

Purple-team feedback loop

A purple-team exercise pairs operator actions with defensive observation. For every step, record prevention, telemetry availability, analytic behavior, alert routing, analyst interpretation, containment, and recovery. Share ATT&CK technique IDs as a common language, but preserve the exact procedure and environment-specific evidence.

The Operation Desert Hydra project shows a CTI-to-detection pipeline where behavior mapping becomes validated analytics instead of a report-only artifact.

From test execution to defensible coverage

Separate “rule exists,” “telemetry exists,” “test executed,” “alert fired,” and “analyst responded.” Each is a different maturity state. Record the test artifact, data source, time window, rule version, result, false-positive considerations, owner, and retest date.

A failed detection is useful evidence when the execution was valid and reproducible. Route it into a telemetry, analytic, workflow, or control backlog rather than hiding it behind an aggregate coverage percentage.

Applied workflow — run a threat-informed purple-team validation

A defensible emulation links source-backed adversary behavior to a relevant organizational hypothesis, a safe procedure, expected telemetry, analytic logic, analyst action, and a retest. It does not copy an actor name onto a generic tool sequence or claim “coverage” because a technique appears in a matrix.

  1. Define the decision and protected process. State which prevention, telemetry, detection, triage, or response capability the exercise will validate and why the behavior matters to this environment.
  2. Build a source-backed behavior set. Use current threat reporting, ATT&CK procedure examples, incident evidence, and local context. Record source date, confidence, platform, identity, software, and procedure—not only technique IDs.
  3. Translate behavior into a safe test. Preserve the observable properties required by the hypothesis while replacing destructive payloads, real targets, sensitive data, and uncontrolled infrastructure with benign equivalents.
  4. Write the telemetry contract. For each action, name the endpoint, identity, network, application, cloud, or audit events expected; required fields; time synchronization; collection path; retention; and query used to confirm ingestion.
  5. Validate atomically first. Execute one bounded procedure, confirm target state, evidence, telemetry, and cleanup, and only then combine tested procedures into a scenario chain.
  6. Observe the complete defensive lifecycle. Record control prevention, raw telemetry, enrichment, analytic evaluation, alert creation, routing, analyst interpretation, escalation, containment, and recovery as separate outcomes.
  7. Classify the gap accurately. Distinguish missing telemetry, malformed fields, delayed ingestion, logic failure, excessive noise, disabled rule, routing failure, playbook weakness, analyst-context gap, or containment limitation.
  8. Improve and retest the same procedure. Version the data source, analytic, test artifact, environment, expected result, and acceptance criteria. Re-execute under comparable conditions and retain both failed and successful evidence.

Validation record

  • Source behavior, procedure, and ATT&CK mapping
  • Environment relevance and hypothesis
  • Test artifact, inputs, target, and cleanup
  • Expected data sources, fields, and timing
  • Prevention, telemetry, analytic, alert, and response results
  • Gap owner, change, acceptance criteria, and retest

Tool selection

Use Atomic Red Team for narrow repeatable procedures, CALDERA or a controlled framework for sequenced emulation, custom scripts when exact behavior or safety needs it, and AdversaryGraph for mapping scenarios to ATT&CK, detections, evidence, and validation results. The procedure—not the platform name—is the unit of evidence.

1200km case-study path

Study Operation Desert Hydra, then use the Attack–Defense Simulation Framework and AdversaryGraph Attack Simulation guide to build a source-to-test-to-detection evidence chain.

Safe lab exercise

Select one behavior relevant to the lab platform, create an ATT&CK Navigator layer, implement a benign atomic test, define expected logs, execute it, query raw events, validate or improve one analytic, record analyst actions, clean up, and rerun the identical versioned test.

Evidence gate: never mark a technique “covered” without a versioned procedure, successful controlled execution, confirmed required telemetry, an evaluated analytic or control, documented analyst/response outcome, and an environment-specific limitation statement.

Module 9 — AI-assisted offensive security and MCP

6 topics

AI can accelerate interpretation and orchestration, but it also adds a non-deterministic planner, new data flows, tool permissions, prompt-injection risk, and supply-chain trust to the engagement.

Appropriate roles for an offensive-security assistant

Useful low-risk roles include summarizing authorized scan output, normalizing evidence, explaining protocols, proposing test cases, comparing requests, drafting reproducible notes, mapping observed behavior to ATT&CK, and suggesting questions for human review. Higher-risk roles include selecting targets, invoking exploit tools, changing infrastructure, moving laterally, or handling credentials.

Treat output as a hypothesis. Models can hallucinate options, misread banners, conflate products, invent CVEs, or omit side effects. The Complete Guide to AI-Driven Penetration Testing places AI inside a reviewable modern workflow rather than presenting autonomy as evidence.

Hub: AI in Offensive Security · The AI Revolution in Offensive Security

MCP, tool orchestration, and HexStrike

Model Context Protocol can expose tools and resources to an AI client. That creates a powerful separation between a reasoning interface and execution services, but the client trusts configured servers and a local server runs with the access available to its process. Tool descriptions are not authorization controls.

HexStrike is the site’s practical research hub for MCP-based offensive workflows. Start with installation and MCP configuration, then study the isolated Kali/Ubuntu/Ollama architecture before attempting a lab scenario.

Project: HexStrike repository · HexStrike documentation

Guardrails for AI-connected security tools

  • Run tools in a dedicated lab or constrained execution container with least privilege and explicit egress rules.
  • Allowlist targets and operations at the tool layer; do not rely on the system prompt to enforce scope.
  • Require human confirmation for network changes, exploitation, credential use, file writes, persistence, lateral movement, and external communication.
  • Separate secrets from prompts and logs; use short-lived scoped credentials and redact evidence sent to remote models.
  • Record the model, prompt context, proposed action, tool call, parameters, result, reviewer, and final decision.
  • Assume retrieved web pages and tool output may contain indirect prompt injection.

OWASP identifies prompt injection and excessive agency as material risks, and the MCP security model places responsibility on clients, servers, and operators to implement access controls, consent, input validation, trusted-server selection, and sandboxing.

Primary: OWASP Prompt Injection · MCP trust model

Testing LLM applications and agents

AI-system red teaming extends application testing with prompt injection, indirect instructions, sensitive-information disclosure, unsafe output handling, retrieval poisoning, excessive agency, tool misuse, identity and privilege boundaries, memory, cross-agent trust, and unbounded resource consumption.

Test the complete system—not only the model. Trace input through retrieval, prompts, policy, tools, authorization, data stores, output rendering, audit, and human approval. The Practical Attacks Against LLM Agents article provides a dedicated lab-oriented treatment.

Primary: OWASP Agentic Security Initiative

Validate AI output and preserve provenance

Keep raw tool output separate from model summaries. Every claim should point to a request, response, scan record, log, screenshot, source document, or reproduced observation. Record uncertainty and discarded suggestions; do not silently convert model confidence into finding severity.

The Nmap meets ChatGPT and Burp Suite with LLMs guides are best used as assisted-analysis patterns: the authoritative evidence remains the scan or proxy record.

Applied workflow — operate an AI + MCP assessment stack safely

An AI-connected offensive toolchain is a privileged automation system. Its risk is determined by the combined model, client, prompts, retrieved content, MCP servers, tool schemas, credentials, execution host, network reach, approval design, and logs. A persuasive chat response is never a substitute for deterministic tool evidence.

  1. Draw the trust and data-flow diagram. Identify the user, client, local and remote models, MCP servers, tool processes, secrets, retrieved sources, target network, storage, logs, and every boundary where assessment data leaves the controlled environment.
  2. Classify tools by effect. Separate read-only interpretation, passive retrieval, active discovery, authentication, file write, code execution, exploitation, credential use, privilege change, persistence, movement, data transfer, and external communication. Assign a default-deny policy to higher-impact classes.
  3. Constrain at the execution layer. Enforce target allowlists, network egress, filesystem mounts, process limits, command or argument validation, timeouts, rate limits, credential scope, sandboxing, and disposable runtime state outside the prompt.
  4. Design explicit approvals. Show the operator the selected target, tool, normalized arguments, expected effect, data being sent, rollback, and evidence location. Require fresh confirmation for state-changing or credential-bearing actions; do not approve an opaque multi-step plan.
  5. Validate schemas and responses. Tool inputs should use narrow types and enumerations, reject unknown fields, normalize identifiers, and return structured status, observations, artifact references, errors, and side effects. Free-form model prose must not become an executable argument without validation.
  6. Assume tool output is untrusted. Web content, repository text, scan banners, documents, and MCP resources may contain indirect instructions. Keep data distinct from system policy and prevent retrieved content from authorizing a tool call.
  7. Record provenance end to end. Log the request ID, user, model and version, system/policy version, prompt context reference, provider, tool server/version, approved call, normalized arguments, raw result hash, parsed result, reviewer decision, and resulting finding or discarded lead.
  8. Test failure and abuse cases. Exercise unavailable servers, malformed schemas, timeouts, duplicate calls, prompt injection, over-broad targets, path traversal, secret leakage, approval bypass, stale context, tool-result spoofing, and partial cleanup before using the stack in an engagement.
  9. Keep the human analytical gate. Independently verify asset attribution, scope, product and version, command safety, CVE relevance, ATT&CK mapping, finding severity, evidence quality, and remediation before acceptance.

HexStrike learning path

  • Install and configure MCP clients
  • Isolate orchestration and execution
  • Run the Metasploitable lab
  • Integrate Burp in a controlled workflow
  • Read the full HexStrike documentation

Approval record

  • Objective, scope reference, user, and target
  • Model proposal and cited source artifacts
  • Tool name, server version, and normalized arguments
  • Effect class, data disclosure, and rollback
  • Human approval or rejection with timestamp
  • Raw result, parser result, evidence hash, and review state

Safe lab exercise

Deploy the AI client and HexStrike server in separate constrained containers or VMs connected only to an isolated vulnerable lab. Allow read-only discovery first. Add one active tool behind an approval gate, then test target rejection, argument validation, timeout, prompt injection in tool output, audit completeness, and teardown.

When not to use AI

Avoid remote models when evidence cannot leave the environment, provenance cannot be retained, targets or authority are ambiguous, output cannot be independently checked, tool effects cannot be constrained, the operator lacks protocol expertise, or the task is deterministic and safer as a reviewed script.

Example assistant contract

The assistant may propose a scoped plan, explain why each step answers the objective, cite source artifacts, and prepare one normalized tool call at a time. It must display uncertainty, refuse out-of-scope targets, never treat retrieved text as authorization, never fabricate successful execution, preserve raw results, and wait for explicit approval before any active or state-changing call.

Evidence gate: no AI-produced observation becomes a finding until an authorized human can trace it to scoped deterministic evidence, reproduce the result, verify the interpretation, and account for every tool effect and disclosed data element.

Module 10 — Laboratories, tool discipline, and reporting

6 topics

Practice should be isolated, observable, reproducible, and tied to defensive learning. Reporting is part of the technical work, not an administrative afterthought.

Design a safe offensive-security laboratory

Use dedicated virtualization or cloud accounts, host-only or tightly controlled networks, synthetic data, snapshots, known-vulnerable targets, central logging, and resource limits. Prevent accidental routing to production or the public internet, and make teardown repeatable. Do not place malware or vulnerable services on a bridge without explicit containment.

The 1200km Lab Work index includes network, web, identity, malware, cloud, and attack-simulation environments. A practical starting point is the vulnerable Ubuntu lab with a full HexStrike walkthrough; Metasploitable is covered in the HexStrike + Codex lab.

Lab rule: use private address space, disable unintended egress, snapshot before exploitation, and verify that no test credentials or payloads are reused outside the lab.

Choose tools by evidence need

  • Recon and network: Nmap, Masscan where rate is explicitly controlled, DNS and certificate tools, Wireshark.
  • Web and API: Burp Suite, OWASP ZAP, browser developer tools, API clients, content discovery and carefully scoped fuzzers.
  • Vulnerability validation: vendor checks, Nuclei templates under review, vulnerability scanners, Metasploit in labs or explicitly authorized validation.
  • Identity: native directory tools, BloodHound-style relationship analysis, Kerberos and certificate assessment tools in controlled environments.
  • Emulation: Atomic Red Team, CALDERA, and scenario-specific scripts with deterministic cleanup.
  • AI orchestration: HexStrike and approved MCP servers inside constrained execution boundaries.

Tools age; methodology and evidence requirements persist. Record versions, configuration, scope, rate, templates or modules, and raw output so results can be reproduced and challenged.

Libraries: PT Tools & Techniques · HexStrike · AI Offensive Security

Write findings that engineering teams can act on

Each finding should include affected scope, security property, preconditions, reproducible evidence, impact, likelihood and confidence, relevant attack path, observed controls, remediation options, validation notes, and cleanup status. Separate root cause from symptom and business impact from generic severity labels.

The engagement report should also answer the original objective: which paths succeeded, which were prevented or detected, where testing stopped, what remains unknown, and what should be prioritized. Include a technical appendix and a consumer-specific executive summary without changing the underlying judgment.

Remediation, detection handoff, and retest

A retest verifies the same affected condition and plausible bypasses after remediation. Record code, configuration, identity, or architectural changes; confirm the security property; and update rather than erase the original evidence. If a compensating control is accepted, state what it prevents or detects and what residual path remains.

Map validated behavior to telemetry and detection work. The CTI-to-detection module and Detection Engineering Techniques provide the blue-team handoff.

Recommended learning order

  1. Networking, operating systems, HTTP, identity, scripting, version control, and evidence handling.
  2. Authorization, ROE, lab isolation, reconnaissance, and manual validation.
  3. Web and API testing with an intercepting proxy and OWASP methodology.
  4. Windows/Linux privilege concepts, Active Directory, cloud identity, containers, and Kubernetes.
  5. ATT&CK-based adversary emulation paired with logging and detection validation.
  6. AI assistance and MCP only after you can independently verify the underlying tool, protocol, command, and result.

A strong portfolio shows reproducible lab design, bounded testing, clear evidence, defensive relevance, and honest limitations. Start with the Basic Toolkit for Penetration Testing, then use the lab index and local article archive to deepen one domain at a time.

Capstone workflow — deliver a reviewable attack–defense assessment

The capstone joins the entire guide into one controlled engagement. The quality measure is not the number of tools or exploits used; it is whether authority, evidence, technical reasoning, defensive value, cleanup, and communication remain intact from the first hypothesis through the retest.

  1. Build the laboratory. Use isolated networks or accounts, synthetic identities and data, snapshots or infrastructure as code, time synchronization, endpoint and identity logging, network visibility, a SIEM, resource limits, and verified teardown.
  2. Write the engagement pack. Define sponsor decision, objective, assumed access, scope, exclusions, permitted techniques, test window, data policy, contacts, stop conditions, expected telemetry, success criteria, and cleanup plan.
  3. Establish a normal baseline. Record expected services, identities, application workflows, cloud resources, logs, alerts, and performance before testing. A lab without known normal state cannot explain what changed.
  4. Map and validate the attack surface. Reconcile inventory, perform controlled discovery, attribute observations, select one reachable hypothesis, and validate only the conditions needed for the objective.
  5. Execute a short scenario. Chain two or three previously validated reversible procedures, maintaining action IDs, evidence references, defensive timestamps, approval gates, and cleanup tasks.
  6. Measure defense at every layer. Record prevention, raw telemetry, analytic behavior, alert enrichment, routing, analyst understanding, containment, and recovery. Do not compress these states into one pass/fail label.
  7. Report for multiple consumers. Produce an executive decision summary, objective result, attack-path narrative, prioritized findings, technical evidence appendix, detection gaps, remediation backlog, limitations, and residual risk.
  8. Remediate and retest. Change the vulnerable condition or control, rerun the same versioned proof under comparable conditions, verify business functionality and telemetry, and retain before/after evidence.
  9. Close and archive responsibly. Verify teardown, revoke credentials, destroy or retain evidence according to policy, disclose unresolved artifacts, capture lessons, and preserve only the sanitized portfolio material approved for publication.

Recommended capstone variants

  • External service → validated web authorization gap
  • Standard identity → risky directory-control path
  • Cloud workload → excessive identity privilege
  • Threat report → ATT&CK scenario → detection retest
  • AI + MCP → constrained lab tool orchestration

Quality rubric

  • Authority and scope are unambiguous
  • Every claim points to preserved evidence
  • Impact and uncertainty are stated honestly
  • Actions are reversible and cleanup is verified
  • Defensive observations are reproducible
  • Retest proves the changed security property

Report architecture

Use a decision summary; engagement authority and limitations; objective results; attack-path timeline; finding records; control and telemetry observations; ATT&CK mappings with procedures; remediation plan; retest criteria; cleanup attestation; and a technical appendix containing sanitized evidence references.

Portfolio publication

Publish only synthetic lab targets and explicitly approved evidence. Remove customer names, real addresses, credentials, internal architecture, unpublished vulnerabilities, and operational details that create avoidable risk. Link the write-up to the relevant 1200km lab, tool guide, and defensive outcome.

Completion gate: the assessment is complete only after every objective has a supported result or stated limitation, every change has a verified cleanup state, findings have owners and retest criteria, and the sponsor receives a decision-ready summary.
