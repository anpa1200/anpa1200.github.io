Understanding Command and Control

Command and Control, often shortened to C2, is the communication that lets an adversary direct or coordinate activity on compromised systems. The channel may deliver instructions, return results, or obtain additional components. It can use familiar protocols and legitimate services, so the protocol name alone does not establish malicious intent.

Typical activities

  • Establish communication between a compromised system and operator-controlled or abused infrastructure.
  • Exchange instructions, execution results, status messages, or additional payloads.
  • Use direct connections, intermediaries, or changing infrastructure to maintain communication as the operation evolves.

Example: A process repeatedly contacts an unfamiliar destination and receives responses before launching commands. Timing, destination history, process identity, and the resulting actions together support a C2 hypothesis.

A domain in a binary, periodic traffic, encryption, or use of port 443 is not sufficient proof of C2. Some payloads operate autonomously after staging and do not need a continuing command channel. Data transferred over C2 may also serve an objective such as exfiltration.

Defender’s question: Who is communicating, what changes after the communication, and what corroborates operator control?

Relevant ATT&CK techniques 8 examples

These curated Enterprise ATT&CK techniques and sub-techniques illustrate behavior relevant to Command and Control. They are not an exhaustive checklist or an official one-to-one mapping. ATT&CK tactics describe goals; the IDs below identify techniques. A technique may appear in more than one stage when the context justifies it.

  • T1071.001Web Protocols

    Sub-technique of T1071 — Application Layer Protocol

    Web protocols can carry command traffic; correlate requests with process identity and subsequent behavior.

  • T1071.004DNS

    Sub-technique of T1071 — Application Layer Protocol

    DNS can carry command-channel traffic; unusual queries require corroboration rather than a protocol-only verdict.

  • T1573.001Symmetric Cryptography

    Sub-technique of T1573 — Encrypted Channel

    Symmetric encryption can conceal command-channel content while metadata and endpoint evidence remain observable.

  • T1573.002Asymmetric Cryptography

    Sub-technique of T1573 — Encrypted Channel

    Asymmetric encryption can protect a command channel; encryption alone is not evidence of malicious control.

  • T1090.003Multi-hop Proxy

    Sub-technique of T1090 — Proxy

    Multiple proxies can separate the compromised host from the operator and complicate infrastructure attribution.

  • T1105Ingress Tool Transfer

    Technique

    A command channel may transfer additional tools or payloads after the first foothold, overlapping delivery and installation.

  • T1102.002Bidirectional Communication

    Sub-technique of T1102 — Web Service

    A legitimate web service can be abused for two-way operator communication.

  • T1041Exfiltration Over C2 Channel

    Technique

    A command channel may also carry stolen data, overlapping communication with actions on objectives.

Verified against MITRE’s official STIX data, Enterprise ATT&CK v20.0, on 2026-10-31. Revoked and deprecated entries are excluded. Mapping notes and pinned source provenance (JSON).

What to look for

Combine DNS, HTTP/TLS, flow metadata, and endpoint context. A suspicious connection alone does not establish C2.

Use the model with care

The classic seven stages of Lockheed Martin’s Cyber Kill Chain are an organizing model, not a mandatory linear sequence. Stages can repeat, overlap, be skipped, or remain unobserved. Fileless, insider, identity, cloud, and AI attacks may not fit every stage. A reading association is not proof that an observed attack completed that stage.

MITRE ATT&CK describes adversary behaviors and tactical goals at a different level of detail. Its tactics are not interchangeable with these stages. The external technique links below are learning associations, not an official one-to-one mapping.

Start with CTI Kill Chain: An Analyst Guide With Real-World Evidence for case evidence and analytic limits, or the original framework reference (PDF).
